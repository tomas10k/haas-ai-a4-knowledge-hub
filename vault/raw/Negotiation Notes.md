# Negotiation Notes

My personal study notes for Negotiations (MBA 252), taught by Professor Solène Delecourt at Berkeley Haas, Fall 2026. Written from the slides of Classes 1 to 5. These notes paraphrase the course material in my own structure; they are not a copy of the slides.

## 1. Why negotiate at all

**People do not negotiate enough.** About one third of MBAs say they never negotiate, 25 percent say they feel "a great deal of apprehension" about it, and some people say they would pay to avoid negotiating, or would rather go to the dentist.

**Why people avoid it:** it seems greedy, it touches ego and self esteem, they fear hurting the relationship, and they do not know their own value.

**Why they should:** the payoff is large and it compounds.

* Negotiating a first salary can be worth about $500K by age 60, compared with a matched job type where the first salary was not negotiated.
* MBA graduates who negotiated their first salary out of the MBA (Carnegie Mellon data) earned about 7.4 percent more.
* Negotiating at every salary change can add up to about $2M over a career.
* Coronary stents: roughly two million are implanted every year, and different hospitals pay different prices, from $700 to $1,600, for the exact same stent from the same supplier. Bargaining ability explains 79 percent of that variation (Grennan, 2014).

**Core mindsets.**

* Always be negotiating. As an MBA graduate you negotiate every day with managers, co workers, clients, vendors and sales reps, plus in personal life.
* Everything is negotiable, almost. There is no such thing as a truly standard process. With a well written letter and patience, most rules can be adjusted; the example was a $2,300 medical bill.
* You need to ask. Others cannot guess what you want unless you tell them.
* You can improve: focus on the process rather than the outcome, develop your own style, and do not copy others.
* "You don't get what you deserve in life, you get what you negotiate."

## 2. What a negotiation is

**Definition used in class:** a process by which parties share information and use problem solving to achieve mutually beneficial solutions.

**Why mutual benefit matters:** the counterpart is more likely to agree, more likely to follow through and implement the deal, your reputation is preserved for future deals, and mutual benefit expands the pie and creates value.

**A successful outcome** means both parties feel they won, there is repeat business, and you keep a good reputation.

## 3. Distributive versus integrative negotiation

| | Distributive | Integrative |
|---|---|---|
| Time horizon | Short term | Long term |
| Result | Win or lose | Win win |
| Goal | Individual gain | Joint gain |
| Interests | Opposed | Congruent |
| Issues | Single issue | Multiple issues |
| Pie | Zero sum, fixed | The pie can grow |

Most real negotiations are integrative, and integrative negotiation is the focus of the course. Be creative by adding more issues to the table. Integrative negotiation requires two skills: **creating value** and **claiming value**.

### Three types of issues

1. **Distributive issues:** both sides value the issue in exactly opposite ways, so the more the counterpart gets, the less you get. Example: price when buying from a vendor.
2. **Compatible issues:** both sides want the same outcome. Example: continuing education and training in a job negotiation.
3. **Integrative issues:** the sides have different priorities across issues, so each can trade what it cares about less for what it cares about more. Example: start date versus vacation time.

### Pareto concepts

* **Pareto improvement:** a change that makes at least one party better off without making anyone worse off.
* **Pareto optimal (efficient):** a point where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. The set of such points is the **Pareto frontier**.

In real life there are no utility points, but you should still map the issues and quantify them, for example in dollars, so you can trade across them.

### Sandbagging

Sandbagging is a feigned concession: pretending to give ground on an issue you actually agree on, to extract a concession elsewhere. It can produce short term gains, but it risks being caught lying, confuses what you actually want, damages relationships when discovered, and signals low power. It is considered suboptimal.

## 4. The three things to know before any negotiation

1. **BATNA, the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement:** what you will actually do if no deal is reached. It is the key concept in negotiation.
2. **Reservation point** (also called resistance point or bottom line): the worst deal you would still accept; below it you walk away.
3. **Aspiration point:** the ambitious outcome you aim for.

You should also try to learn the counterpart's BATNA, reservation point and aspiration point.

**BATNA versus reservation point.** They are related but not always the same. The relationship depends on your preferences. If your BATNA equals your reservation point, you are indifferent between the deal and walking away. In the Yerba Mate case, valuing the Guayaki deal at $8,400 would mean valuing it exactly the same as the Vitale Mate alternative, which may not be true. Your reservation point should generally not be set lower than your BATNA's value.

**Power comes from perceived BATNA strength.** How strong you believe your BATNA is becomes your biggest source of power and confidence. In the Yerba Mate case, buyers had a strong BATNA while sellers had a "phantom" BATNA, one that was not real.

**BATNA example from a pop quiz.** You hold a signed PM offer from Snowflake at $180K base and are in final rounds with Google, which has not made an offer yet. Your current BATNA with Snowflake is not the Google job; an unrealized offer is a phantom BATNA. If Google then offers $210K, your BATNA rises to $210K.

**ZOPA, the Zone of Possible Agreement:** the bargaining range, meaning the overlap between the two parties' acceptable ranges, bounded by their resistance points. A barely positive ZOPA leaves very little room for a deal.

**The paradox of ambition.** Higher aspirations tend to produce objectively better outcomes, but you may feel less satisfied with the result. Keep it in mind and celebrate the effort.

## 5. Preparation: the prep sheet

The course prep sheet covers:

* Goals and interests (the purpose behind what you want)
* Value creating options
* Aspiration point package and resistance point package
* BATNA
* Sources of power
* Comparables, meaning objective criteria
* Strategy, including how to build rapport

Ask what you really want. Social norms and other people's projections shape aspirations (Correll, 2004), so check whether your goals are your own.

## 6. Building rapport

Do not jump straight to the point. Start with informal conversation ("schmoozing") on personal or social topics. This is not only about being pleasant: in an experiment where people were randomly assigned to build rapport first or to negotiate straight away, the rapport group was more likely to reach a deal.

Conversation is a learnable skill. Before a conversation, prepare a few bullet points on topics to explore: what you know about the person, what they said last time, their company or public profile. The book *TALK* by Alison Wood Brooks covers this skill in depth.

## 7. First offers

**Make the first offer when possible.** In class data, first offers and final deals were strongly correlated (r = 0.60, p < .001, R² about 0.36): the first offer anchors the outcome.

**How to make a first offer:**

* Make it ambitious enough, optimistic but realistic.
* Present a package, not a single number, and never a range.
* Do not make it immediately; build rapport and check assumptions first.
* Avoid a "chilling effect" from an offer so extreme it ends the conversation.

**Justify it.** The Langer, Blank and Chanowitz (1978) "copy machine" experiment showed that giving a reason, even a weak one, raises compliance. A justified offer ("here is why this package is good for both of us") seems more serious, feels fairer, makes the other party more likely to adjust, and lets you talk about your analysis rather than the number.

**When not to make the first offer:**

* In salary negotiations.
* When you know less than the other party, invite them to "name your price."
* Corollary: if you think the counterpart knows nothing, invite them to go first.

## 8. Concessions

**Rules:**

* Do not set your reservation point too close to your aspiration point; leave room to concede.
* Never make unilateral concessions. Ask for reciprocity.
* Label your concessions so they are recognized.
* Do not say "this is my first and final offer."
* Use silence and patience.
* Treat sharing information as a concession too.
* Do not negotiate with yourself.

**Good concessions are principled** (with a clear rationale), **reciprocated** (earned through effort, which makes them more appreciated), and **small**, shrinking as you approach your reservation point.

**Avoid two extremes:** not negotiating at all out of fear of hurting the relationship, and fighting over every penny until the relationship is damaged. Agreeing with resentment can hurt the relationship too. Negotiations are almost always a repeated game, and standing your ground does not threaten the relationship if done right.

## 9. Responding to a first offer

**Extreme first offer:**

* Do not play their game or discuss their number; talk about your resistance point, not their aspiration point.
* Do not counteranchor with an equally extreme number, which leaves both sides meeting in the middle of an artificial range.
* Take back the power with a question, for example "Unfortunately, I can never do that. Do you still want to talk?" or "Let's talk about a package that would work for both of us."
* Wipe the offer off the table, then present your own justified package or stay silent and let them negotiate with themselves.
* Give them ways to save face.

**Reasonable first offer:**

* Do not ask where it came from, and do not express disgust or incredulity.
* Do not immediately counter; deflect first.
* Do not immediately accept, to avoid the **winner's curse**, the feeling that you left value on the table because the offer was accepted too easily.

## 10. Salary and job offer negotiation

**Offers presented as non negotiable are often negotiable anyway.** In class survey data, 60 percent of offers were presented as non negotiable. Of those, 57 percent of people negotiated and 65 percent of those succeeded. Of offers not presented that way, 88 percent negotiated and 80 percent succeeded.

**Why firms negotiate:** price discovery, competing for talent, benchmarking data, the cost of recruiting, tailoring the offer to make you happier and more productive, and retention.

**Why salary compounds.** Inflation adjustments, performance bonuses and raises are all calculated as a percentage of base salary. Example with 5 percent annual increases: not negotiating gives $200,000, $210,000 and $220,500 over three years (about $630K); negotiating 8 percent more gives $216,000, $226,800 and $238,140 (about $680K). The difference is about $50K in three years.

**Salary is only one piece.** Negotiable items include salary, signing bonus, start date, equity, job title, location, team, remote work, relocation stipend, performance bonus, time off and visa support. For equity, understand RSUs (restricted stock units) versus stock options, the strike price, the vesting schedule (often a one year cliff), and the time window to exercise options.

**The job negotiation process:** prepare, receive the offer, counteroffer, then accept or reject, and celebrate. When receiving an offer: express thanks and some excitement (not too much), confirm the position, request the offer in writing and any missing information, and set a time for the next conversation, since you need time to evaluate. The honeymoon feeling of getting an offer is real but wears off, so negotiate anyway.

**Evaluating an offer:** do I like the job, what are my requirements and priorities, and what is my BATNA? Avoid paralysis from too many options.

**Justifying a counteroffer.** The strongest argument is the **market rate**, the most competitive argument in the fight for talent. Your current salary is irrelevant. Sources include Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Payscale, Salary.com, and talking to people, for example asking "What is a typical compensation package for that position?" Comparables must be comparable by industry and/or role, and it helps to show the data in a spreadsheet. Also argue what you need to be productive and why the employer needs you specifically.

**Weak arguments:** family situation (legitimate for location, weak for salary), cost of living, and tax rates, since everyone faces them.

**Who to ask about pay.** Because of structural pay inequality and homophily ("birds of a feather flock together"), ask people who may be at the top of the earnings range, even if they do not share your background. Salary history bans exist because asking "what do you make now?" perpetuates inequality.

**Multiple offers.** Nobody likes being used as a token. Be honest about which offer you prefer and what would make you accept, for example: "I would like to get to yes with X. If these changes were made, I would accept."

**People, not firms.** You negotiate with people who have personal priorities. In person beats video, which beats phone, because it is easier to say no over the phone. For remote negotiations, agree on a time, eliminate distractions, behave as if in person, and always confirm the outcome by email.

**Opening lines:** "I am very enthusiastic about the company and the position... The benefits package looks great, but I have a few concerns... The base salary is lower than the market rate for similar positions; what can we do about this?"

**Before accepting:** did they treat you well, is the culture a fit, is the company financially stable, are there growth opportunities and a career path, and are there red flags? Recruiters say: do not lie, do not give ultimatums, do not squeeze, and do not focus only on salary. Remember that emails last forever and can be forwarded.

**Integration example from the internship case.** If the candidate had already won Seattle but the company preferred Omaha, the candidate could offer: "I know we agreed I would go to Seattle, but how about I go to Omaha and you pay me $11,000 more in salary?" That creates value and lets the candidate claim about 75 percent of it.

**Basic financial literacy.** Only about 29 percent of Americans understand the three core concepts:

* **Inflation:** about 3 percent a year. Money in a typical 0.01 percent savings account loses purchasing power; high yield savings at about 3.5 percent roughly break even after inflation and taxes.
* **Compound interest:** at 10 percent a year, money doubles in about 7 years. The S&P 500 has returned about 10 percent a year on average over the last century, and $1,000 invested 60 years ago would be worth over $400,000 today.
* **Diversification:** a single company's stock is riskier than a diversified fund such as an S&P 500 index.

If you negotiate an extra $1,000: if you need it soon, use a high yield savings account or CD; if not, invest it in diversified stocks and leave it.

## 11. Fairness

**The ultimatum game.** One player proposes how to split $10 in a single, final offer; the other accepts or rejects, and a rejection leaves both with nothing. The "homo economicus" answer is to offer $1 and accept anything positive, but real people reject offers they see as unfair.

**Three kinds of fairness:** equality (equal shares), equity (shares proportional to contribution), and need (shares based on need).

**Procedural justice.** People judge fairness by whether they were respected and heard. Check in ("Does this seem fair to you?") and listen actively ("Can you tell me your perspective on this?").

**When negotiating:** understand how each party defines fair, frame your preferred outcome so it feels fair to the counterpart, and make the process itself feel fair.

## 12. Weak BATNAs, information and "irrational" negotiators

**Handling a weak BATNA:** improve it, create value first, focus on the relationship, redirect attention to the counterpart's BATNA, or do not negotiate.

**The Teddy Roosevelt story.** In 1912 his campaign printed 3 million pamphlets using a photograph it did not have rights to. Rather than ask permission from a weak position, the campaign manager, Perkins, told the photography studio that using its photo would be great publicity and asked how much the studio would pay. The studio, Moffett, replied that it would be pleased to pay $250.

**Grand Strand case lesson.** A seller who openly shares the real interest (a one year retreat and a need for a job on return) lets the buyer craft a more creative, value expanding offer, such as including employment. Share interests, but not unnecessary vulnerabilities such as personal problems.

**"Irrational" negotiators.** "When you label someone irrational, you limit your own options" (Malhotra and Bazerman, 2007). They may have different information, a different perspective, or hidden interests. Ask: "Why do you care about this? Here is the information as I see it; how do you see it?" Use **perspective taking** (what would I do in their situation?) and **perspective getting** (asking them for their view).

## 13. Team negotiations

**Benefits of a team:** more expertise and ideas, roles that handle complexity, greater acceptance of the agreement, and more perceived power. **Costs:** coordination effort, internal competition and less trust.

**Before using a team, consider** the time available, the complexity of the deal, and the strength of team boundaries.

**Organizing the team:** plan roles in advance, present a united front, use caucuses (private team breaks), and agree on signals. Suggested roles: a lead authorized to make or reject offers, a secondary negotiator who keeps the lead on track or takes over, and a process or strategy person who talks less and analyzes more.

## 14. Anger and emotions (Myti-Pet case)

**The case.** Myti-Pet, a small but fast growing Chicago dry pet food maker, negotiated with Rawmat, its main meat flour supplier. Rawmat had been using leftover scraps, watering the flour and overcharging (the current price was $357 per ton), while Myti-Pet was hiding that its meat flour needs would quadruple in two years and that it had a large wheat flour business Rawmat wanted. Myti-Pet negotiators were instructed to display anger.

**Anger's costs:** it escalates conflict, biases perception, increases impasses, reduces joint gains, reduces understanding of the counterpart's interests, increases offer rejection and retaliation, and can cost more for women and minorities. **Possible benefits:** extracting concessions, feeling more powerful, and motivating action; suppressing anger can also be maladaptive. Distinguish tactical anger (respond to the power move) from genuine anger (respond with sincerity).

**How to de escalate:** take breaks, change the lead negotiator, remove an overly emotional team member, show you could match the anger but choose not to escalate, and point out that both sides can keep blaming each other or move toward a mutually agreeable solution. Learn to de escalate without making too many concessions.

**Power and the future.** Rawmat looked powerful as the large supplier, but Myti-Pet's growth and wheat flour business gave it leverage. Working together beats arguing over rights and who would win in court. **Negotiating the future is usually a good way to get the past taken care of.** A good solution included Rawmat reimbursing up to $200K of retooling, Myti-Pet paying at least part of the $250K for the last shipment, volume pricing (meat flour as low as $328 per ton at 34K tons by year 5; wheat flour as low as $125 per ton immediately above 80K tons), a three year contract covering both wheat and meat flour, tighter water and fat specifications with penalties, and a new delivery schedule.

## 15. Giving feedback

Feedback after each practice negotiation is part of the learning. Motivate rather than demoralize, focus on something that can improve, and be real, honest, direct and helpful. Avoid "failure to warn": people are sometimes too nice to avoid seeming prejudiced (Crosby and Monin, 2007). Look for the common thread in feedback over several weeks rather than overreacting to one session.

## 16. Key takeaways checklist

* Make the first offer when possible, and justify it with objective criteria.
* Know your BATNA, strengthen it, and research the counterpart's.
* Define and focus on your aspiration point; know your reservation point and when to walk away.
* Present packages, not single issues, and trade across issues to create value.
* Make concessions that are principled, reciprocated and shrinking; use silence and patience.
* Build rapport and share information, but do not dive into contentious issues first.
* Make the process feel fair; check in and listen.
* Handle anger by de escalating toward problem solving.
* End the negotiation positively; it is almost always a repeated game.
