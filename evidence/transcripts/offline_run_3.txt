[S1] score 5.92  raw/Designing Financial Models Notes.md > 6. Step 2a: Estimate (ballpark before building) > Strategic rounding
    Pick numbers that make the next operation easy ("compatible numbers"), not simply the nearest number. Friendly numbers: 5, 10, 20, 25, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1,000.

    Examples: $5.50 × 4 becomes $5 × 5 = $25 rather than $6 × 4 = $24, because 25 is easier to reuse. $23.40 × 1,850 becomes $25 × 2,000 = $50K.

    **Offset the rounding:** when I round one factor up, round the other down so errors do not compound. In the ZipCar sketch, 4 hours × $5.50 became 5 hours × $5.00 = $25, and 22 miles × $0.40 became 20 miles × $0.50 = $10. Monthly trips of 880 rounded up to 1,000, so the annual multiplier was rounded down from 12 to 10.

[S3] score 5.42  raw/Negotiation Notes.md > 7. First offers
    **Make the first offer when possible.** In class data, first offers and final deals were strongly correlated (r = 0.60, p < .001, R² about 0.36): the first offer anchors the outcome.

    **How to make a first offer:**

    * Make it ambitious enough, optimistic but realistic.
    * Present a package, not a single number, and never a range.
    * Do not make it immediately; build rapport and check assumptions first.
    * Avoid a "chilling effect" from an offer so extreme it ends the conversation.

    **Justify it.** The Langer, Blank and Chanowitz (1978) "copy machine" experiment showed that giving a reason, even a weak one, raises compliance. A justified offer ("here is why this package is good for both of us") seems more serious, feels fairer, makes the other party more likely to adjust, and lets you talk about your analysis rather than the number.

    **When not to make the first offer:**

    * In salary negotiations.
    * When you know less than the other party, invite them to "name your price."
    * Corollary: if you think the counterpart knows nothing, invite them to go first.

[S5] score 4.11  raw/Designing Financial Models Notes.md > 6. Step 2a: Estimate (ballpark before building) > The six step estimation process
    1. **Sketch:** identify the structure and the simplifying formula (usually P × Q for revenue, or a sum of cost categories for expenses).
    2. **Identify the inputs:** write the raw numbers from the case or my own estimates on the sketch.
    3. **Compute in chunks:** work bottom up, compute small pieces, label each intermediate result.
    4. **Round to compatible numbers:** round aggressively to friendly numbers built from 2s, 5s and 10s. Rounding more is better than skipping the estimate.
    5. **Annualize or aggregate:** convert per period figures to the model's time frame.
    6. **Sanity check:** does the final number make sense, and is it the right order of magnitude?

[S5] score 4.07  raw/Negotiation Notes.md > 10. Salary and job offer negotiation
    * **Inflation:** about 3 percent a year. Money in a typical 0.01 percent savings account loses purchasing power; high yield savings at about 3.5 percent roughly break even after inflation and taxes.
    * **Compound interest:** at 10 percent a year, money doubles in about 7 years. The S&P 500 has returned about 10 percent a year on average over the last century, and $1,000 invested 60 years ago would be worth over $400,000 today.
    * **Diversification:** a single company's stock is riskier than a diversified fund such as an S&P 500 index.

    If you negotiate an extra $1,000: if you need it soon, use a high yield savings account or CD; if not, invest it in diversified stocks and leave it.
