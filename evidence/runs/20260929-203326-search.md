**[S1]** `raw/Negotiation Notes.md` > 9. Responding to a first offer (score 10.9)

> **Extreme first offer:**
> 
> * Do not play their game or discuss their number; talk about your resistance point, not their aspiration point.
> * Do not counteranchor with an equally extreme number, which leaves both sides meeting in the middle of an artificial range.
> * Take back the power with a question, for example "Unfortunately, I can never do that. Do you still want to talk?" or "Let's talk about a package that would work for both of us."
> * Wipe the offer off the table, then present your own justified package or stay silent and let them negotiate with themselves.
> * Give them ways to save face.
> 
> **Reasonable first offer:**
> 
> * Do not ask where it came from, and do not express disgust or incredulity.
> * Do not immediately counter; deflect first.
> * Do not immediately accept, to avoid the **winner's curse**, the feeling that you left value on the table because the offer was accepted too easily.

**[S2]** `raw/Negotiation Notes.md` > 7. First offers (score 9.76)

> **Make the first offer when possible.** In class data, first offers and final deals were strongly correlated (r = 0.60, p < .001, R² about 0.36): the first offer anchors the outcome.
> 
> **How to make a first offer:**
> 
> * Make it ambitious enough, optimistic but realistic.
> * Present a package, not a single number, and never a range.
> * Do not make it immediately; build rapport and check assumptions first.
> * Avoid a "chilling effect" from an offer so extreme it ends the conversation.
> 
> **Justify it.** The Langer, Blank and Chanowitz (1978) "copy machine" experiment showed that giving a reason, even a weak one, raises compliance. A justified offer ("here is why this package is good for both of us") seems more serious, feels fairer, makes the other party more likely to adjust, and lets you talk about your analysis rather than the number.
> 
> **When not to make the first offer:**
> 
> * In salary negotiations.
> * When you know less than the other party, invite them to "name your price."
> * Corollary: if you think the counterpart knows nothing, invite them to go first.

**[S3]** `raw/Negotiation Notes.md` > 16. Key takeaways checklist (score 4.23)

> * Make the first offer when possible, and justify it with objective criteria.
> * Know your BATNA, strengthen it, and research the counterpart's.
> * Define and focus on your aspiration point; know your reservation point and when to walk away.
> * Present packages, not single issues, and trade across issues to create value.
> * Make concessions that are principled, reciprocated and shrinking; use silence and patience.
> * Build rapport and share information, but do not dive into contentious issues first.
> * Make the process feel fair; check in and listen.
> * Handle anger by de escalating toward problem solving.
> * End the negotiation positively; it is almost always a repeated game.

**[S4]** `raw/Negotiation Notes.md` > 10. Salary and job offer negotiation (score 3.14)

> **The job negotiation process:** prepare, receive the offer, counteroffer, then accept or reject, and celebrate. When receiving an offer: express thanks and some excitement (not too much), confirm the position, request the offer in writing and any missing information, and set a time for the next conversation, since you need time to evaluate. The honeymoon feeling of getting an offer is real but wears off, so negotiate anyway.
> 
> **Evaluating an offer:** do I like the job, what are my requirements and priorities, and what is my BATNA? Avoid paralysis from too many options.
> 
> **Justifying a counteroffer.** The strongest argument is the **market rate**, the most competitive argument in the fight for talent. Your current salary is irrelevant. Sources include Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Payscale, Salary.com, and talking to people, for example asking "What is a typical compensation package for that position?" Comparables must be comparable by industry and/or role, and it helps to show the data in a spreadsheet. Also argue what you need to be productive and why the employer needs you specifically.
> 
> **Weak arguments:** family situation (legitimate for location, weak for salary), cost of living, and tax rates, since everyone faces them.

**[S5]** `raw/Negotiation Notes.md` > 8. Concessions (score 3.06)

> **Rules:**
> 
> * Do not set your reservation point too close to your aspiration point; leave room to concede.
> * Never make unilateral concessions. Ask for reciprocity.
> * Label your concessions so they are recognized.
> * Do not say "this is my first and final offer."
> * Use silence and patience.
> * Treat sharing information as a concession too.
> * Do not negotiate with yourself.
> 
> **Good concessions are principled** (with a clear rationale), **reciprocated** (earned through effort, which makes them more appreciated), and **small**, shrinking as you approach your reservation point.
> 
> **Avoid two extremes:** not negotiating at all out of fear of hurting the relationship, and fighting over every penny until the relationship is damaged. Agreeing with resentment can hurt the relationship too. Negotiations are almost always a repeated game, and standing your ground does not threaten the relationship if done right.
